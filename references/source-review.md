# Fit-for-purpose source review

Evaluate a source by what it can show, for this question, in this context. Do not use a numeric prestige score and do not down-rank a source merely because it is unofficial.

## Match source roles to claims

| Source role | Often useful for | Common limits to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Official policy, specification, filing, or documentation | What an organization says, requires, supports, or measured under stated conditions | Self-interest, implementation gaps, changing versions, narrow definitions |
| Academic paper or dataset | Methods, measurements, causal or comparative analysis, prior literature | Sample and setting limits, publication lag, model assumptions, replication status |
| Journalism or specialist analysis | Events, interviews, synthesis, investigation, industry context | Editorial framing, source access, compressed technical detail |
| Company or practitioner material | Operational detail, firsthand implementation, product behavior | Commercial incentives, selective cases, undocumented conditions |
| Forum, SNS, app review, social platform, or community post | Lived experience, reactions, complaints, workarounds, discourse, emergent behavior | Self-selection, unverifiable identity, platform bias, missing denominator, coordinated or repeated content |
| Aggregator, index, or search result | Discovery and navigation | Usually not evidence until the original item is opened |

One source may serve several roles. State which role is being used.

## Original-page and quotation checks

Treat search-result snippets, aggregators, summaries, and quoted reposts as discovery aids until the original item is opened. Check the original page for important sources and consequential claims when accessible.

For a direct quotation, confirm that the normalized quote appears as a contiguous passage in the original content and preserve the author or organization, publication or update date, checked date, and useful locator when available. Do not turn this into character-by-character verification of every report sentence; prioritize direct quotations, consequential claims, and sources that materially shape the conclusion.

## Social and community sources

Treat social material as primary evidence when the topic is what people said, experienced, complained about, celebrated, or learned to work around.

- Preserve enough context to avoid changing the meaning.
- Record platform, date, apparent relationship to the subject, and relevant thread context when available.
- Describe examples as examples. A few posts cannot establish population prevalence, incidence, or consensus.
- Distinguish repeated independent experiences from reposts, coordinated campaigns, quoted stories, and one viral source.
- Note selection, survivorship, moderation, authenticity, demographic, and algorithmic exposure limits.
- Use authoritative sources for policy or technical correctness when that is the claim; use community sources to show reception or lived behavior.

## Independence and duplication

Canonicalize URLs when practical, strip tracking parameters, and group sources that are not substantively independent.

Treat these as one evidence family unless there is a clear independent contribution:

- syndications, mirrors, reposts, and translations of the same item;
- articles that only repeat one press release, filing, interview, study, dataset, or social post;
- multiple pages owned by the same actor making the same unsupported assertion;
- summaries whose only evidence is another source already in the ledger.

Shared ownership alone does not automatically erase distinct firsthand observations, but record the relationship. Source-surface diversity and independent evidence families are different facts; report both honestly.

## Contradictions

Do not average away disagreements.

1. Check that the sources use the same terms, population, timeframe, baseline, and outcome.
2. Look for changes over time, local variation, sample selection, incentives, methods, and implementation differences.
3. Record which claims truly conflict and which apply under different conditions.
4. Search for material counterevidence before settling a consequential conclusion.
5. Present unresolved conflicts and explain what evidence would resolve them.

## Freshness

Record when the source was published or updated and when it was checked. For policies, prices, product behavior, schedules, and fast-moving events, prefer current original material and flag superseded pages. Older sources remain useful for history, baselines, or durable concepts when labeled accordingly.

## High-risk claims

For medical, legal, financial, safety, security, strong causal, and consequential numeric claims:

- use current authoritative or original evidence for the normative or measured claim;
- cross-check material claims when feasible;
- preserve units, population, timeframe, baseline, uncertainty, and measurement conditions;
- separate reported fact, interpretation, and recommendation;
- make jurisdiction, applicability, and professional-advice limits explicit;
- use social sources for experience or discourse, not as the sole basis for technical correctness or population rates.

This is personal research assistance, not an audit or regulatory evidence system. Increase care without pretending the workflow supplies certification.

## Exclusion criteria

Exclude a source only when it is:

- inaccessible or effectively empty after reasonable original-page checking;
- materially misrepresented by the note or unable to support the stated use;
- irrelevant to the research question;
- clearly fabricated, impersonated, or manipulated beyond useful interpretation;
- a duplicate that adds no independent content or context.

Otherwise retain it, classify its suitable use, and record limitations. Weak support for one claim may still be strong primary evidence for another, such as public reaction or a product complaint.
